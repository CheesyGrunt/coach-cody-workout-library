The part nobody says out loud

Every coach you look up to was, at some point, standing on a wet deck with no idea what to write on the board. That's not imposter syndrome. That's the actual starting line, and everybody crosses it.

The good news is that swimming is a craft with a shape to it. Practices aren't invented from nothing — they follow patterns, and once you can see the patterns you can build your own. That's what the 473 practices in this library are for. Not to run word-for-word forever, but to show you the shape until you can draw it yourself.

You will write a bad practice. Probably in your first month. The kids survive it, you learn what didn't work, and you write a better one Thursday. That's the job.

How a practice is built

Almost every workout in this library follows the same skeleton. Learn this and you can read any practice on the site — or any practice a coach hands you.

Warm-Up500–800
Get blood moving and shoulders loose. Nothing hard. This is also where you find out who slept and who didn't. Vary it — a warm-up that never changes is a warm-up nobody thinks about.
Kick400–800
Legs are the engine and the first thing to quit in a race. Board, streamline, vertical, social — rotate it so it doesn't become a chore they mail in.
Skill / Pre300–600
Drills, sculling, underwaters, turns. Do this before they're tired. Technique taught to an exhausted swimmer doesn't stick.
Main Set1000–2000
The point of the day. Everything above this is preparation for it. If you only plan one thing well, plan this. It should have a clear job — aerobic, race pace, speed — not a little of everything.
Pull300–600
Buoy, sometimes paddles. Gives the legs a break while the arms keep working. Good spot for breathing patterns.
Blocks / Cool Down200–400
Starts and relay takeovers if you've got time, then easy swimming. Never skip the cool down. It's where the hard work gets absorbed, and it's the last thing they remember about practice.

Add it up and a solid hour-long age group practice lands around 2,800–3,000 meters. If your plan says 6,000, you've written a two-hour practice.

The shorthand

Swim practices are written in a dialect. None of it is complicated once somebody translates it — but nobody ever does, so here.

Reading a set
8×100 @ 1:30
Eight one-hundreds, leaving every minute thirty. Swim it in 1:20 and you get ten seconds rest. Swim it in 1:29 and you get one. The interval controls the difficulty, not the distance.
SCY / SCM / LCM
Short course yards (25y), short course meters (25m), long course meters (50m). The single most important label on a practice. Long course has half the walls, so the same set is far harder.
A / B / C
Interval columns by lane speed. A is your fastest group, C is your developing group. Same set, different clocks.
Descend 1–4
Each one faster than the last. Number four is the fastest. Descend means the times go down, not the effort.
Build
Get faster within a single swim. Start easy at the wall, finish fast at the other end.
Negative split
Second half faster than the first. Teaches pacing better than almost anything else you can do.
Broken swim
A race distance cut into pieces with short rest — a 200 as 4×50 on :10 rest. Lets them feel race speed before they can hold it straight through.
O / E
Odds and evens. “O: free, E: stroke” means alternate every other rep.
Strokes and order
FR / BK / BR / FL
Free, back, breast, fly.
IM
Individual medley. All four strokes, in order: fly, back, breast, free.
IMO
IM order. A set done fly, then back, then breast, then free — usually one round each.
CH
Choice. Swimmer picks the stroke.
KDS
Kick, drill, swim. Usually 25 of each inside a 75.
Underwaters and walls
UW
Underwater. The stretch off the wall before they surface — often the fastest part of the whole race.
DK / DKOB
Dolphin kick / dolphin kick on back.
Breakout
The first stroke coming out of the underwater. Timing it well is worth more than most swimmers realize.
Streamline / Position 11
Hands stacked, arms locked behind the ears, body long and quiet. Position 11 is the same idea with arms shoulder-width — reaching through the lats.
The 15
Fifteen meters. The legal limit for staying underwater off a wall or start. Past it and it's a DQ.
No-breath breakout
No breathing on the first stroke or two out of the wall. Uncomfortable, and exactly what races require.
Effort and energy
Aerobic / EN1
Comfortable, sustainable, conversational-ish. Builds the base everything else sits on.
Threshold / EN2
Comfortably hard. The fastest pace they could hold for a long time. This is where fitness gets built.
VO2 max
Hard, short, real rest. Trains the top end of the aerobic system. Should hurt, with form still intact.
Race pace
Exactly the speed of their goal race. Not faster, not slower. Teaches the body what the race feels like.
SP1–SP5
Speed zones, easy to all-out. SP5 is a full sprint with full rest.
DPS
Distance per stroke. Cover more water with each pull. “DPS − 1” means do it in one fewer stroke than usual.
Hypoxic
Restricted breathing — every 3, 5, 7 strokes. Use it sparingly and watch them closely. Nobody does breath-holding alone or on the bottom.
Season words
Base
Early season. Volume, aerobic work, technique. Building the engine.
Build
Mid season. Intensity climbs, volume holds. Race-specific work enters.
Taper
Final weeks. Volume drops hard, speed stays sharp. The rest is where the fitness finally shows up.
Test set
A repeated benchmark set. Run it every few weeks — same set, same conditions — and you can prove progress instead of guessing.

Your first practice on deck

Pick one from the library that matches your course and time, and run it. Here's how to not make it harder than it is:

  1. Filter to your course first. SCY, SCM, or LCM — up top on the library page. Running a short course practice in a long course pool is the fastest way to have a bad day.
  2. Read it all the way through before you get on deck. Out loud, if that helps. You want to already know what comes after the main set when a kid asks.
  3. Write it on the board before they get in. Whole practice, not one set at a time. Swimmers who can see where they're going pace themselves better and ask fewer questions.
  4. Pick intervals you can loosen. If half the lane is missing the send-off, add five seconds. Nobody gets faster failing an interval for forty minutes.
  5. Explain the why of the main set in one sentence. “This is about holding your 200 pace when you're tired.” That's it. Kids work harder when the set has a point.
  6. Watch one thing. Not everything — one thing. Walls, or breakouts, or stroke count. Fix that. Next practice, watch something else.
  7. Cool them down and end on time. Both matter more than you'd think, and both build trust with swimmers and with parents in the lobby.

If you want a starting point and don't want to choose, the library has a “Pick one for me” button. Filter to your course, hit it, run what comes up.
